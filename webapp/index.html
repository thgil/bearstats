01

What happened?

Twice as many sightings as any year before, and thirteen deaths.

Four years, one map.

Sightings reported in each prefecture, played from FY2022 to FY2025. The country lights up from the north.

It has a centre.

Akita and Iwate alone reported 23,331 of the 50,801 sightings — nearly half. The map shows raw counts, so big rural prefectures run dark partly on size, but the gap here is not size.

It happened in autumn.

Most years the curve peaks in June and falls away. Autumn 2025 broke it: 15,998 reports in October alone, more than the whole of FY2022. The beech and oak crop had failed across Tohoku, and bears that would have fed in the hills came down for apples, persimmons and rubbish — and found towns. By November the government had sent soldiers to Akita.

Up close.

Tohoku's prefectures do not publish individual reports. Four prefectures on the Sea of Japan coast and north of Tokyo do: Toyama, Niigata, Gunma and Saitama. Here is their year, one month at a time. April: 64 reports. October: 1,795. Yellow marks are people hurt.

The cost.

Thirteen people killed by bears in a single year. The previous worst since records began in 2008 was six.

02

Is it over?

No. Every month of the new year is running well above the record year.

Same months, new year.

April 1,787 against 800. May 4,581 against 2,528. June 6,260 against 4,227.

Sixty-seven percent ahead.

By the end of June FY2026 had logged 12,628 sightings, against 7,555 at the same point of the record year.

More bears seen. Not, so far, more people hurt.

Injuries in April–July: 53 this year, 55 last. Deaths: 6 against 4 — a gap too small to read.

03

Will it get worse?

That depends on the acorns, and nobody knows yet.

Spring does not tell you much about what autumn will bring. FY2024 was running ahead of FY2025 at the end of June — 7,601 sightings against 7,555 — and still finished the year at 20,513, only 40% of FY2025's eventual total. A fast start can fade; a slow one can explode.

What has changed is the baseline underneath both years. Japan's bear population roughly tripled between 2012 and 2025, from an estimated 15,000 to around 54,000. Over the same years the people licensed to hunt them aged out of the job: more than 70,000 active bear hunters in the 1970s, fewer than 20,000 by 2025. More bears, fewer hunters — that does not reset every April.

What decides whether autumn 2026 repeats autumn 2025 is the mast crop — the beech and oak nuts bears fatten on before winter. A second failed crop would send hungry bears back into towns; a good one would ease the pressure. Neither is visible yet: the trees fruit from September.

Check back in November.

Methods

A “sighting” here is any report made to a prefectural government of a bear seen, tracked, or otherwise encountered — not a confirmed animal. The same bear can generate several reports, and reporting rates vary with how alarmed people are, so year-on-year swings partly reflect that as well as bear activity.

Only four prefectures — Toyama, Niigata, Gunma and Saitama — publish individual reports as open data; the other 42, including Akita and Iwate, publish totals only. Hokkaido's point-data feed begins in May 2026 and is excluded from the month-by-month replay above.
